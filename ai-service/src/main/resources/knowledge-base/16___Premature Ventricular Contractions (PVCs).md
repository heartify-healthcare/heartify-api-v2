Premature ventricular contractions (PVCs) are a type of irregular heartbeat.

They occur when the electrical signal that starts your heartbeat comes

from one of your bottom two heart chambers (ventricles). The signal

typically starts in the top right chamber (atrium).

PVCs are not always a problem. But if they repeatedly happen for months

or years, they can cause a type of [cardiomyopathy,](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/16841-cardiomyopathy) or heart muscle

weakening. PVCs usually go away with medication or other minimally


invasive treatments.

Some people call premature ventricular contractions “pre ventricular

contractions.” Healthcare providers may also refer to them as:

Premature ventricular complexes.

Ventricular extrasystoles.

Ventricular premature beats.
#### How do electrical signals control your heartbeat?

Typically, in your heart [conduction](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/body/21648-heart-conduction-system) system, a cluster of cells in your right

atrium called the sinoatrial (SA) node sends out the first electrical signal of

a [heartbeat.](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/articles/17064-heart-beat) That signal moves through your heart and travels to your left

and right ventricles. It tells your heart to contract.

In PVCs, that first signal starts in one of your ventricles. As a result, the

signal travels differently through your heart and causes an irregular

heartbeat. Sometimes, a PVC may feel like your heart “skipped a beat.”

## Diagnosis and Tests
### How are premature ventricular contractions

Your healthcare provider asks you about your symptoms and health history


and performs a physical exam. They may listen to your heartbeat with a

stethoscope to check for signs of irregularities.

Usually, the first test is an [electrocardiogram](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diagnostics/16953-electrocardiogram-ekg) (EKG). An EKG measures your


[heart](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diagnostics/17402-pulse--heart-rate) rate and evaluates the electrical signals in your heart. Your provider

usually can see an [arrhythmia](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/16749-arrhythmia) or extra beats clearly on an EKG.

Sometimes, you may wear a Holter [monitor](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diagnostics/21491-holter-monitor) or another type of heart


monitor to measure your heart’s electrical activity over several days.

Measuring your heart rhythm and heart rate over a few days can help

detect infrequent PVCs.
